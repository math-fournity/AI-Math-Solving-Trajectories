# Proof

## Answer

**No.** The map is not necessarily surjective for sufficiently large $p$.

## Counterexample

Let $G = \mathrm{SL}_2$ (split, simply-connected, semisimple) acting on $R = \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ via the standard representation:
$$
\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}).
$$

This is a linear algebraic action of $G(\mathbb{Z}) = \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ on the finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-algebra $R$.

### Step 1: $R^{G(\mathbb{Z})} = \mathbb{Z}$

$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$ is generated by
$$
T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.
$$

**$T$-invariance:** $T$ sends $(x,y) \mapsto (x+y, y)$, so any invariant $f \in \mathbb{Z}[x,y]$ satisfies $f(x+y, y) = f(x,y)$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{Z}$. Setting $y = 1$: $f(x+1, 1) = f(x, 1)$ for all $x$, so $f(x,1)$ is constant in $x$. More generally, for any fixed $y_0 \neq 0$, $f(x + y_0, y_0) = f(x, y_0)$ implies $f(\cdot, y_0)$ is periodic with period $y_0$; since $f$ is a polynomial, $f(\cdot, y_0)$ must be constant. Thus $f(x, y) = g(y)$ for some polynomial $g \in \mathbb{Z}[y]$ (the case $y = 0$ follows by polynomiality).

**$S$-invariance:** $S$ sends $(x,y) \mapsto (-y, x)$, so $f(-y, x) = f(x, y)$, i.e., $g(x) = g(y)$ for all $x, y$ with $y \neq 0$ and $x \neq 0$. This forces $g$ to be constant.

Therefore $R^{G(\mathbb{Z})} = \mathbb{Z}$, and
$$
R^{G(\mathbb{Z})} \otimes \mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{F}_p.
$$

### Step 2: For every prime $p$, $(R \otimes \mathbb{F}_p)^{G(\mathbb{F}_p)} \supsetneq \mathbb{F}_p$

Define the **Dickson-type invariant**:
$$
F_p(x, y) = x^p y - x\, y^p \in \mathbb{F}_p[x, y].
$$

**Claim:** $F_p$ is $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$-invariant.

*Proof.* For $g = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \in \mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)$, the action gives $(x, y) \mapsto (ax + by,\; cx + dy)$. Using the Frobenius endomorphism ($t^p = t$ for $t \in \mathbb{F}_p$, hence $a^p = a$, $b^p = b$, etc.):
$$
\begin{aligned}
g \cdot F_p &= (ax + by)^p(cx + dy) - (ax + by)(cx + dy)^p \\
&= (a^p x^p + b^p y^p)(cx + dy) - (ax + by)(c^p x^p + d^p y^p) \\
&= (ax^p + by^p)(cx + dy) - (ax + by)(cx^p + dy^p) \\
&= ac\, x^{p+1} + ad\, x^p y + bc\, y x^p + bd\, y^{p+1} \\
&\quad - ac\, x^{p+1} - ad\, x\, y^p - bc\, x^p y - bd\, y^{p+1} \\
&= (ad - bc)(x^p y - x\, y^p) \\
&= \det(g) \cdot F_p = F_p. \qquad \checkmark
\end{aligned}
$$

**$F_p$ is non-constant:** $F_p = xy(x^{p-1} - y^{p-1})$ has degree $p + 1 \geq 3$, so $F_p \notin \mathbb{F}_p$.

Therefore, for every prime $p$:
$$
(R \otimes \mathbb{F}_p)^{G(\mathbb{F}_p)} = \mathbb{F}_p[x,y]^{\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)} \supsetneq \mathbb{F}_p \ni F_p.
$$

### Step 3: The map is not surjective for any $p$

The map in question is:
$$
\phi_p : R^{G(\mathbb{Z})} \otimes \mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{F}_p \longrightarrow (R \otimes \mathbb{F}_p)^{G(\mathbb{F}_p)} = \mathbb{F}_p[x,y]^{\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{F}_p)}.
$$

Its image is $\mathbb{F}_p$ (the constants), but the target contains the non-constant element $F_p$. Hence $\phi_p$ is **not surjective** for any prime $p$.

In particular, it is not surjective for sufficiently large $p$.

## Key Observation

The essential reason the map fails to be surjective is that $G(\mathbb{F}_p)$ is a **finite group**, not the algebraic group $G_{\mathbb{F}_p}$. Over $\mathbb{F}_p$, a finite subset is never Zariski dense in a positive-dimensional variety, so $G(\mathbb{F}_p)$-invariants can be strictly larger than $G_{\mathbb{F}_p}$-invariants (scheme-theoretic invariants).

The Frobenius endomorphism creates polynomial invariants of the finite group $G(\mathbb{F}_p)$ — such as $x^p y - xy^p$ — that have no analogue in characteristic $0$ or for the algebraic group. These "Frobenius invariants" exist for **every** prime $p$ (with degree growing as $p+1$), so increasing $p$ does not eliminate them.

$$
\boxed{\text{No}}
$$
