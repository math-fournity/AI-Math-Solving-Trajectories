# Proof: $MK = 0$ when $K$ is a nil ideal and $M$ is a minimal right ideal

**Claim.** Let $R$ be a ring (not necessarily with identity or commutative). Suppose $K$ is a nil ideal of $R$, and $M$ is a minimal right ideal of $R$. Then $MK = 0$.

**Answer:** $\boxed{\text{Yes, } MK = 0.}$

---

## Proof

We work throughout without assuming $R$ has a $1$ or is commutative.

### Step 1. $MK$ is a right ideal contained in $M$.

Recall $MK = \{\sum_i m_i k_i : m_i \in M,\, k_i \in K\}$ (finite sums).

- **$MK \subseteq M$.** Since $M$ is a right ideal, $m_i k_i \in M$ for each $i$, so every finite sum $\sum m_i k_i \in M$.
- **$(MK)R \subseteq MK$.** For $x = \sum m_i k_i \in MK$ and $r \in R$,
  $$xr = \Big(\sum m_i k_i\Big) r = \sum m_i (k_i r).$$
  Since $K$ is a right ideal, $k_i r \in K$, so each $m_i(k_i r) \in MK$, hence $xr \in MK$.

Thus $MK$ is a right ideal of $R$ with $MK \subseteq M$. By minimality of $M$,
$$MK = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad MK = M.$$

### Step 2. Suppose for contradiction that $MK = M \neq 0$.

Pick $m_0 \in M$ and $k_0 \in K$ with $m_0 k_0 \neq 0$, and set $x = m_0 k_0 \in M$.

Consider the cyclic right $R$-submodule of $M$ generated by $x$. Since $R$ need not have $1$, this submodule is
$$N = \mathbb{Z}\,x + xR = \{n\,x + x r : n \in \mathbb{Z},\; r \in R\} \subseteq M.$$
As a right $R$-module, $N$ is nonzero (it contains $x \neq 0$) and $N \subseteq M$. Since $M$ is a minimal right ideal, $M$ is a simple right $R$-module, so its only nonzero submodule is $M$ itself. Therefore
$$N = M.$$

Now $xR \subseteq N = M$ is a right $R$-submodule of $M$ (it is closed under right multiplication by $R$ and under addition). By simplicity of $M$,
$$xR = 0 \quad\text{or}\quad xR = M.$$

We treat the two cases separately.

### Step 3. Case 1: $xR = M$.

Since $m_0 \in M = xR$, there exist $r_1, \dots, r_s \in R$ with
$$m_0 = \sum_{j} x\, r_j = \sum_j (m_0 k_0) r_j = m_0\Big(\sum_j k_0 r_j\Big).$$
Set $k' = \sum_j k_0 r_j$. Because $K$ is a right ideal, $k_0 r_j \in K$ for every $j$, so $k' \in K$.

Thus $m_0 = m_0 k'$. Iterating,
$$m_0 = m_0 k' = (m_0 k')k' = m_0 (k')^2 = \cdots = m_0 (k')^n \quad\text{for all } n \ge 1.$$

But $k' \in K$ and $K$ is a nil ideal, so $k'$ is nilpotent: $(k')^N = 0$ for some $N$. Hence
$$m_0 = m_0 (k')^N = m_0 \cdot 0 = 0.$$

This contradicts $m_0 k_0 = x \neq 0$. **Case 1 is impossible.**

### Step 4. Case 2: $xR = 0$.

Then $N = \mathbb{Z}\,x + xR = \mathbb{Z}\,x = M$, so every element of $M$ is of the form $n\,x$ for some $n \in \mathbb{Z}$.

For any $n\,x \in M$ and any $r \in R$,
$$(n\,x)\,r = n\,(xr) = n \cdot 0 = 0,$$
since $xR = 0$. Therefore $Mr = 0$ for all $r \in R$, i.e. $MR = 0$.

In particular $MK \subseteq MR = 0$, so $MK = 0$. But this contradicts our assumption $MK = M \neq 0$. **Case 2 is also impossible.**

### Step 5. Conclusion.

Both cases arising from $MK = M$ lead to contradictions. Therefore $MK \neq M$, and by the dichotomy of Step 1 we must have
$$MK = 0. \qquad \blacksquare$$

## Remark (conceptual route via the Jacobson radical)

The result also follows from the standard structural theory, valid for rings without identity:

1. **Nil elements are quasi-regular.** If $y^n = 0$, set $z = \sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(-y)^i \in R$. A direct telescoping computation gives $y + z + yz = 0$, so $y$ is right quasi-regular. Since $K$ is a (two-sided) ideal, $rx \in K$ is nilpotent hence quasi-regular for every $x \in K$, $r \in R$. By the quasi-regular characterization of the Jacobson radical (which does not require $1$), $K \subseteq J(R)$.

2. **$J(R)$ annihilates every simple right $R$-module.** For a simple module $S$: if $S R = 0$ the annihilation is trivial. Otherwise some $s \in S$ has $sR = S$, and $\operatorname{ann}(s)$ is a modular maximal right ideal (modular via a $c$ with $sc = s$), so $J(R) \subseteq \operatorname{ann}(s)$, giving $s\,J(R)=0$ and hence $S\,J(R)=0$.

3. A minimal right ideal $M$ is a simple right $R$-module, so $M\,J(R) = 0$, and since $K \subseteq J(R)$,
$$MK \subseteq M\,J(R) = 0.$$

Both routes confirm $MK = 0$.

### PROOF COMPLETE
