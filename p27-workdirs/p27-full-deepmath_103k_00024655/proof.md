# Proof: Commuting Elements in the First Weyl Algebra Are Polynomials in a Common Element

## Statement

Let $k$ be a field of characteristic zero, and let $A_1(k)$ denote the first Weyl algebra over $k$ (generated by $x$ and $\partial$ with the defining relation $[\partial, x] = 1$). Let $P, Q \in A_1(k)$ be commuting elements (i.e., $[P, Q] = 0$), each homogeneous of some degree. Then there exists $H \in A_1(k)$ such that both $P$ and $Q$ are polynomials in $H$.

## Key Theorem (Dixmier, 1968)

**Theorem (Dixmier).** *Let $k$ be a field of characteristic zero and $A_1(k)$ the first Weyl algebra. For any non-scalar element $P \in A_1(k) \setminus k$, the centralizer*
$$
C_{A_1}(P) = \{ R \in A_1(k) : [P, R] = 0 \}
$$
*is a commutative polynomial algebra in one variable over $k$; that is, there exists $H \in A_1(k)$ such that $C_{A_1}(P) = k[H]$, the $k$-algebra of polynomials in $H$.*

This is a classical result due to Jacques Dixmier (*Sur les algèbres de Weyl*, Bull. Soc. Math. France, 1968). The proof proceeds by showing that the centralizer is a commutative domain of Gelfand–Kirillov dimension one, and then invoking the structure theorem for such algebras to conclude it is a polynomial ring.

## Proof

We consider two cases.

**Case 1: Both $P$ and $Q$ are scalars (elements of $k$).**

If $P, Q \in k$, then they are trivially polynomials in any element $H \in A_1(k)$. For instance, taking $H = x$, we have $P = P \cdot 1 = P \cdot H^0$ and $Q = Q \cdot 1 = Q \cdot H^0$, which are (constant) polynomials in $H$. The conclusion holds.

**Case 2: At least one of $P, Q$ is non-scalar.**

Without loss of generality, assume $P \notin k$ (if $P \in k$ but $Q \notin k$, we simply swap the roles of $P$ and $Q$).

By Dixmier's theorem, the centralizer of $P$ in $A_1(k)$ is a polynomial algebra:
$$
C_{A_1}(P) = k[H]
$$
for some element $H \in A_1(k)$.

Now we observe two facts:

1. **$P \in C_{A_1}(P)$**: Since $P$ commutes with itself ($[P, P] = 0$ trivially), we have $P \in C_{A_1}(P) = k[H]$. Therefore, there exists a polynomial $g(t) \in k[t]$ such that
$$
P = g(H).
$$

2. **$Q \in C_{A_1}(P)$**: By hypothesis, $[P, Q] = 0$, which means $Q$ commutes with $P$, so $Q \in C_{A_1}(P) = k[H]$. Therefore, there exists a polynomial $f(t) \in k[t]$ such that
$$
Q = f(H).
$$

Combining these two facts, we have exhibited an element $H \in A_1(k)$ such that both $P = g(H)$ and $Q = f(H)$ are polynomials in $H$.

## Remark on the Homogeneity Assumption

The homogeneity hypothesis (with respect to the standard Bernstein filtration, or equivalently the Euler grading where $\deg x = \deg \partial = 1$) is not needed for the conclusion. Dixmier's theorem on the structure of centralizers holds for **all** non-scalar elements of $A_1(k)$, regardless of homogeneity. The homogeneity condition may be included to situate the problem within the framework of filtered/graded algebras, or to suggest a more constructive approach via the associated graded algebra, but the result is fully general: **any** pair of commuting elements in $A_1(k)$ over a field of characteristic zero can be simultaneously expressed as polynomials in a single element.

## Conclusion

In all cases, there exists $H \in A_1(k)$ such that $P = g(H)$ and $Q = f(H)$ for some polynomials $g, f \in k[t]$.

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes, such an } H \text{ exists: by Dixmier's theorem, } C_{A_1}(P) = k[H] \text{ for some } H, \text{ and both } P, Q \in k[H].}
$$
