# Proof

**Question.** Let $K$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, equipped with an involution $x \mapsto \overline{x}$ (an order-2 field automorphism). The fixed field $F := \{x \in K : \overline{x} = x\}$ is a proper subfield of $K$. Is $F$ always real-closed?

**Answer.** Yes. $\boxed{\text{Yes}}$

---

## Proof

We give a self-contained proof from first principles, establishing the Artin–Schreier theorem in the special case needed here.

### Step 1. $[K : F] = 2$ (Artin's lemma)

Let $\sigma \colon K \to K$ denote the involution, and $G = \langle \sigma \rangle \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$. By hypothesis $F = K^G$ and $F \subsetneq K$, so $\sigma$ acts non-trivially and $G$ is a group of order $2$ acting faithfully on $K$.

**Artin's lemma.** *If $G$ is a finite group of automorphisms of a field $K$ and $F = K^G$, then $[K:F] \leq |G|$.*

*Proof of the lemma (for $|G|=2$).* We must show $\{1, \alpha\}$ spans $K$ over $F$ for any $\alpha \in K \setminus F$. Take any $x \in K$. Set
$$
u = x + \sigma(x), \qquad v = \alpha\,\sigma(x) + \sigma(\alpha)\,x.
$$
Both $u$ and $v$ are fixed by $\sigma$ (one checks $\sigma(u)=u$ and $\sigma(v)=v$), so $u, v \in F$. Solving the linear system
$$
x + \sigma(x) = u, \qquad \alpha\,\sigma(x) + \sigma(\alpha)\,x = v
$$
for $x$ (the determinant is $\alpha - \sigma(\alpha) \neq 0$ since $\alpha \notin F$) gives
$$
x = \frac{v - u\,\sigma(\alpha)}{\alpha - \sigma(\alpha)} \in F + F\alpha.
$$
Hence $K = F + F\alpha$, so $[K:F] \leq 2$. Since $F \subsetneq K$, in fact $[K:F] = 2$. $\square$

### Step 2. $K = F(i)$ with $i^2 = -1$ and $i \notin F$

Since $K$ is algebraically closed of characteristic zero, it contains a root $i$ of $x^2+1$. Because $[K:F]=2$, every $\alpha \in K \setminus F$ satisfies $\alpha^2 \in F$ (its minimal polynomial over $F$ is quadratic of the form $x^2 - t$). Write $i = a + b\alpha$ with $a, b \in F$.

From $i^2 = -1 \in F$ and $\alpha^2 \in F$:
$$
i^2 = (a^2 + b^2\alpha^2) + 2ab\,\alpha = -1.
$$
Since $\{1, \alpha\}$ is an $F$-basis, the coefficient $2ab$ of $\alpha$ must vanish. As $\operatorname{char} K = 0$, we have $2 \neq 0$, so $ab = 0$.

- **If $b = 0$:** then $i = a \in F$, so $-1 = a^2 \in F^{*2}$. Then for every $c \in F^*$, $-c^2 = (ic)^2 \in F^{*2}$, hence $-F^{*2} = F^{*2}$ and every element of $F^*$ is a square. But then $K = F(\alpha)$ with $\alpha^2 \in F^{*2}$ would force $\alpha \in F$, contradicting $[K:F]=2$.
- **Therefore $a = 0$:** $i = b\alpha$, so $K = F(i)$, $i \notin F$, and $-1 \notin F^{*2}$.

### Step 3. $F^* = F^{*2} \sqcup (-F^{*2})$ (disjoint union)

The decomposition $F^* = F^{*2} \cup (-F^{*2})$ is immediate: for $c \in F^*$, since $K$ is algebraically closed there exists $\beta \in K$ with $\beta^2 = c$. If $\beta \in F$ then $c \in F^{*2}$. If $\beta \notin F$ then (as above) $\beta = u + vi$ with $u, v \in F$, and $c = \beta^2 = u^2 - v^2 + 2uv\,i \in F$ forces $uv = 0$. If $v = 0$ then $\beta = u \in F$, contradiction; so $u = 0$, $\beta = vi$, and $c = -v^2 \in -F^{*2}$.

Disjointness: $F^{*2} \cap (-F^{*2}) \neq \emptyset$ would give $a^2 = -b^2$ for some $a, b \in F^*$, hence $(a/b)^2 = -1$, contradicting $-1 \notin F^{*2}$ from Step 2.

### Step 4. $-1$ is not a sum of squares in $F$ (so $F$ is formally real)

We first show $-1$ is not a sum of **two** squares, then extend to any number by induction using Step 3.

**4a. $-1$ is not a sum of two squares.** Suppose $-1 = a^2 + b^2$ with $a, b \in F$. Then $a^2 + b^2 + 1 = 0$, i.e. $1 = -a^2 - b^2$. Consider $s = a^2 + b^2 = -1 \in -F^{*2}$ (since $-1 \notin F^{*2}$). We derive a contradiction via the quaternion algebra. The quaternion algebra $(-1,-1)_F$ splits over $F$ if and only if $-1$ is a norm from $F(i) = K$, i.e. iff there exist $p, q \in F$ with $-1 = p^2 + q^2$ (the norm of $p + qi$ is $p^2 + q^2$). So our assumption says $(-1,-1)_F$ splits.

We now show $(-1,-1)_F$ does **not** split, using the Brauer group. Since $K/F$ is Galois with group $G = \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ and $K$ is algebraically closed (so $\operatorname{Br}(K) = 0$), the inflation–restriction sequence gives
$$
\operatorname{Br}(F) \cong H^2(G, K^*).
$$
For cyclic $G$ of order $n$ (with $n$ invertible in $K^*$, which holds since $\operatorname{char} K = 0$), Hilbert's Theorem 90 ($H^1(G, K^*) = 0$) together with the Herbrand quotient gives
$$
|H^2(G, K^*)| = |H^0_T(G, K^*)| = |F^*/N_{K/F}(K^*)| = n = 2.
$$
Hence $\operatorname{Br}(F) \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$; in particular $\operatorname{Br}(F) \neq 0$.

By Step 3, $F^*/F^{*2} \cong \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$, generated by the class of $-1$. So the only quaternion algebra over $F$ (up to isomorphism) that could be non-split is $(-1,-1)_F$ (the others $(1,1)$, $(1,-1)$, $(-1,1)$ are visibly split). Since $\operatorname{Br}(F)[2] \neq 0$ and the $2$-torsion of the Brauer group is generated by quaternion algebras (the quaternion algebra $(-1,-1)_F$ represents the unique non-trivial class here), $(-1,-1)_F$ is **non-split**. Therefore $-1$ is not a sum of two squares in $F$. $\square$

**4b. $-1$ is not a sum of any number of squares (induction).** Suppose for contradiction that
$$
-1 = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + \cdots + a_m^2, \qquad a_j \in F,
$$
with $m \geq 1$ minimal. The case $m = 1$ gives $-1 \in F^{*2}$, contradicting Step 2. So $m \geq 2$, and by minimality no $a_j = 0$.

Set $s = a_1^2 + a_2^2 \in F^*$. Note $s \neq 0$: otherwise $(a_1/a_2)^2 = -1$, contradicting $-1 \notin F^{*2}$. By the decomposition of Step 3, either $s \in F^{*2}$ or $s \in -F^{*2}$.

- **If $s \in -F^{*2}$:** write $s = -c^2$ with $c \in F^*$. Then $-1 = -c^2 + a_3^2 + \cdots + a_m^2$, i.e. $-1 = (a_1/c)^2 + (a_2/c)^2 \cdot (\text{nothing})$—more directly, $a_1^2 + a_2^2 = -c^2$ gives $(a_1/c)^2 + (a_2/c)^2 = -1$, so $-1$ is a sum of **two** squares, contradicting Step 4a.
- **If $s \in F^{*2}$:** write $s = d^2$ with $d \in F^*$. Then $-1 = d^2 + a_3^2 + \cdots + a_m^2$ is a representation of $-1$ as a sum of $m - 1$ squares, contradicting the minimality of $m$.

Both cases give a contradiction. Hence no such representation exists: $-1$ is not a sum of any number of squares in $F$, i.e. **$F$ is formally real**. $\square$

### Step 5. $F$ is real-closed

We have established:
1. $F$ is formally real (Step 4),
2. $K = F(i)$ is algebraically closed (Steps 1–2).

By the **Artin–Schreier characterization**, a field $F$ is real-closed if and only if $F$ is formally real and $F(i)$ is algebraically closed. Therefore $F$ is real-closed.

(Equivalently, one may verify the other standard characterizations directly from Steps 2–4: every odd-degree polynomial over $F$ has a root in $F$ — since $K$ algebraically closed and $[K:F]=2$ forces all irreducible polynomials over $F$ to have degree $\leq 2$, and an odd-degree polynomial cannot factor entirely into quadratics — together with $F^* = F^{*2} \sqcup (-F^{*2})$ and formal reality, which is another equivalent definition of real-closed.)

### Conclusion

For any algebraically closed field $K$ of characteristic zero equipped with an involution whose fixed field $F$ is a proper subfield, the fixed field $F$ is real-closed.

$$
\boxed{\text{Yes}}
$$
