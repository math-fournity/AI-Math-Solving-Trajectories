# Proof: $G$-invariant ideals are definable over the fixed field

## Statement

Let $K/k$ be a finitely generated field extension with $k = K^G$ for some set $G$ of automorphisms of $K$. Let $k[X_1,\ldots,X_n] \subset K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be the induced extension of polynomial rings, and let $I \triangleleft K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ be a $G$-invariant ideal (i.e., $\sigma(I) = I$ for every $\sigma \in G$, where $\sigma$ acts on coefficients). Then

$$I = \bigl(I \cap k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]\bigr)\cdot K[X_1,\ldots,X_n].$$

## Proof

### Step 1. $K/k$ is a finite Galois extension.

Let $H = \langle G \rangle$ be the subgroup of $\operatorname{Aut}(K)$ generated by $G$. Then $K^H = K^G = k$ (an element is fixed by every $\sigma \in G$ iff it is fixed by the group they generate). By Artin's theorem, $K/K^H = K/k$ is a Galois extension; in particular it is **algebraic**. Since $K/k$ is both finitely generated and algebraic, it is **finite**. A finite Galois extension has finite Galois group, so $H = \{\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_d\}$ with $d = |H| = [K:k]$. Since $I$ is $G$-invariant and $H$ is generated by $G$, $I$ is $H$-invariant: $\sigma_i(I) = I$ for all $i$.

### Step 2. Setup and a key invertible matrix.

Set $A = k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ and $B = K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$. Choose a $k$-basis $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_d$ of $K$ (so $d = [K:k]$). Define the $d \times d$ matrix

$$M = \bigl(\sigma_i(\beta_j)\bigr)_{1 \le i,j \le d} \in M_d(K).$$

**Claim:** $\det(M) \neq 0$.

*Proof of claim.* Consider $M \cdot M^{\mathsf{T}}$. Its $(i,k)$-entry is

$$\sum_{j=1}^d \sigma_i(\beta_j)\,\sigma_k(\beta_j) = \sum_{j=1}^d \sigma_j(\beta_i\,\beta_k) = \operatorname{Tr}_{K/k}(\beta_i \beta_k),$$

where the last equality uses the fact that $\{\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_d\}$ is the full Galois group and the trace is the sum over all Galois conjugates. (More explicitly: as $j$ ranges over $1,\ldots,d$ and we sum $\sigma_i(\beta_j)\sigma_k(\beta_j)$, we can rewrite this using the fact that $\sigma_i$ is an automorphism: $\sigma_i(\beta_j)\sigma_k(\beta_j) = \sigma_j(\sigma_j^{-1}\sigma_i(\beta_j) \cdot \sigma_j^{-1}\sigma_k(\beta_j))$... A cleaner way: the matrix $M^{\mathsf{T}} M$ has $(i,k)$-entry $\sum_j \sigma_j(\beta_i)\sigma_j(\beta_k) = \operatorname{Tr}_{K/k}(\beta_i \beta_k)$, which is the **discriminant matrix** of the basis $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_d$.)

Since $K/k$ is separable (being Galois), the trace form $(x,y) \mapsto \operatorname{Tr}_{K/k}(xy)$ is non-degenerate, so $\det\bigl(\operatorname{Tr}_{K/k}(\beta_i\beta_j)\bigr) \neq 0$. Therefore

$$\det(M)^2 = \det(M \cdot M^{\mathsf{T}}) \neq 0,$$

which gives $\det(M) \neq 0$. $\square$

### Step 3. Decomposition of elements of $B$.

Since $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_d$ is a $k$-basis of $K$, every polynomial $f \in B = K[X_1,\ldots,X_n]$ can be written **uniquely** as

$$f = \sum_{j=1}^d \beta_j\, f_j, \qquad f_j \in A = k[X_1,\ldots,X_n].$$

(Each coefficient of $f$ in $K$ is uniquely a $k$-linear combination of $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_d$; collecting terms gives the $f_j$.)

### Step 4. Galois action and inversion.

Apply $\sigma_i$ to $f$ (acting on coefficients):

$$\sigma_i(f) = \sum_{j=1}^d \sigma_i(\beta_j)\, f_j = \sum_{j=1}^d M_{ij}\, f_j.$$

In matrix form, with $\mathbf{f} = (f_1, \ldots, f_d)^{\mathsf{T}}$ and $\boldsymbol{\sigma}(f) = (\sigma_1(f), \ldots, \sigma_d(f))^{\mathsf{T}}$:

$$\boldsymbol{\sigma}(f) = M\, \mathbf{f}.$$

Since $M$ is invertible (Step 2),

$$\mathbf{f} = M^{-1}\, \boldsymbol{\sigma}(f), \qquad \text{i.e.,} \quad f_j = \sum_{i=1}^d (M^{-1})_{ji}\, \sigma_i(f).$$

### Step 5. The main argument.

The inclusion $(I \cap A)\cdot B \subseteq I$ is immediate: $I \cap A \subseteq I$ and $I$ is an ideal of $B$.

For the reverse inclusion, let $f \in I$. By $G$-invariance (hence $H$-invariance), $\sigma_i(f) \in I$ for every $i$. By Step 4,

$$f_j = \sum_{i=1}^d (M^{-1})_{ji}\, \sigma_i(f).$$

Each $(M^{-1})_{ji}$ lies in $K \subset B$ (a constant polynomial), and each $\sigma_i(f) \in I$. Since $I$ is an ideal of $B$,

$$f_j \in I \quad \text{for every } j = 1, \ldots, d.$$

But $f_j \in A$ by construction (Step 3). Therefore $f_j \in I \cap A$ for every $j$. Consequently,

$$f = \sum_{j=1}^d \beta_j\, f_j \in (I \cap A)\cdot B.$$

This shows $I \subseteq (I \cap A)\cdot B$, completing the proof. $\blacksquare$

## Answer

$$\boxed{\text{Yes. } I = \bigl(I \cap k[X_1,\ldots,X_n]\bigr)\cdot K[X_1,\ldots,X_n].}$$
